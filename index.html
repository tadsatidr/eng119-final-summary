Skimming and Scanning6 pts

General guide, not from class slides

Part I · A: choose the best heading for each part (3 pts, ½ each). B: fill in a chart with information from the article (3 pts, ½ each).

The class handouts you sent (Finding Main Ideas; Patterns of Organization) do not cover skimming and scanning, so this part is a general guide, not from class slides. If your teacher gave a skimming/scanning handout, follow it where the two differ.

Skimming vs scanning

Skimming = reading for the gist (อ่านเร็วเอาใจความรวม)

  • Goal: the general idea of a text or a part of it
  • Read: title, first and last paragraph, the first sentence of each paragraph, bold or repeated words
  • Skip: examples, numbers, long details
  • Used for: choosing headings, main idea questions

Scanning = searching for a specific fact (กวาดตาหาข้อมูลเฉพาะ)

  • Goal: one piece of information (a name, a date, a number, a place)
  • Method: decide what the answer looks like (a year? a capital letter? a unit like m²?), then move your eyes quickly until you spot it
  • Then: read the sentence around it carefully to confirm
  • Used for: filling in the chart, detail questions

A. Choosing headings: method

  1. Read all headings firstUnderline the key word in each. Expect 1–2 extra headings that fit nowhere.
  2. Skim one partRead its first and last sentence. Ask: what is this whole part about?
  3. Match the main ideaPick the heading that covers the whole part, not one detail in it.
  4. Do the easy ones firstCross out used headings, then go back to the hard parts.
  5. Final checkEach heading should be used only once (unless the instructions say otherwise).
Heading traps: (1) a heading that repeats a word from the paragraph but not its idea; (2) a heading that matches only one detail (too narrow); (3) a heading that is about the whole article (too broad). A good heading is really the topic or main idea of that part. This links directly to Understanding Paragraphs.

B. Filling in the chart: method

  1. Study the chartRead column and row titles. These are your scanning keywords.
  2. Predict the answer typeYear? Number + unit? Name? A short phrase?
  3. Scan for the row keywordFind the name or place first, then the fact nearby.
  4. Copy exactlyWrite short answers using the text's own words, and spell them correctly.
  5. Check the rowDoes the fact belong to this row (not the next farm or person)?
Exam strategy (6 pts, about 17% of the time): skim the whole article once (1–2 min), do the headings, then use the chart's labels to scan. Answers in the chart are usually short: a word, a number or a phrase. Don't write full sentences. If the chart already has a filled example row, copy its style.

Farming on the Roof

Part 1By 2050, about two out of every three people in the world are expected to live in cities. As cities grow, open land disappears under houses, roads and shopping centres, and fresh food has to travel longer distances to reach city tables. Some city planners believe part of the answer is right above our heads. Across Asia, flat roofs that once held only water tanks and air-conditioners are being turned into farms.

Part 2Growing plants on top of buildings is not new. Ancient writers described gardens on raised terraces in Babylon more than 2,000 years ago, although historians still argue about whether they really existed. In the 1970s, Germany began encouraging “green roofs” covered with grass and small plants to protect buildings from rain and sun. What is different today is the goal: modern rooftop farms are designed to produce food that people actually buy and eat.

Part 3A layer of soil and plants works like a blanket. In hot weather, it can keep the floors below several degrees cooler, so the building uses less electricity for air-conditioning. The plants also catch rainwater that would otherwise rush into drains and cause flooding, and they give birds and insects a place to live in the middle of the concrete.

Part 4Rooftop farms come in many shapes. SkyHarvest in Singapore, opened in 2015, grows leafy greens such as lettuce and kale in tall towers of water instead of soil on 1,200 square metres of a car-park roof. In Bangkok, a university started the Green Crown farm in 2018; its 5,500 square metres of terraced rice fields also slow down rainwater. Tokyo’s Ueno Leaf, the smallest of the three at 450 square metres, has grown tomatoes and herbs on top of a department store since 2012 and sells them in the store’s food hall.

Part 5Not every roof can become a farm, however. Wet soil is heavy, and many older buildings were never designed to carry the extra weight. Water pipes, safety rails and lifts for carrying tools and harvests all add to the cost, which can reach several thousand baht per square metre before the first seed is planted. Strong winds and the intense afternoon sun can also damage crops that would grow easily on the ground.

Part 6Even so, supporters are optimistic. Several cities now offer tax discounts to building owners who add green roofs, and new buildings can be designed with farming in mind from the start. Rooftop farms will never replace the countryside, but they may become a normal part of how cities feed themselves.

Taking Smarter Study Breaks

Part 1Many students believe that the longer they sit at their desks, the more they will learn. In fact, attention tends to drop after a long period of focused work, and tired students often read the same line again and again without taking it in. Short, planned breaks help the brain stay fresh. But not all breaks are equally useful.

Part 2The simplest break is to stand up and move. A five-minute walk, a few stretches or a climb up the stairs increases blood flow and wakes up the body. Students who move during their breaks often say they feel less sleepy when they return to their books.

Part 3Looking at something green can also help. Even ten minutes in a park, or simply looking out of a window at trees, gives the eyes a rest from screens, and many people say it makes them feel calmer.

Part 4A third type of break involves food and water. A glass of water and a light snack such as fruit or nuts can restore energy. Heavy meals, on the other hand, may make students feel sleepy, so a big plate of fried rice is better saved for after study time.

Part 5The most popular break is also the most dangerous: scrolling through social media. A “quick look” at a phone can easily turn into thirty minutes, and the brain does not really rest, because it is still taking in new information.

Part 6To get the most out of breaks, plan them. One well-known method, the Pomodoro Technique, uses 25 minutes of work followed by a 5-minute break, with a longer break of 15 to 30 minutes after four rounds. Setting a timer makes it easier to start working again on time.

Understanding Paragraphs7 pts

From class handouts

Part I · A: choose the most appropriate topic or main idea (1 pt each). B: write the topic, the main idea and the organizational pattern of a paragraph (3 pts).

Topic, main idea, details

Topic (หัวเรื่อง)

Who or what the paragraph is about. A word or phrase, not a sentence. E.g. “the formation of a star”.

Main idea (ใจความสำคัญ)

The author’s main point about the topic. A complete sentence. It is the thought present from the beginning to the end.

Supporting details

Sentences that support, prove, explain, give more information or give examples of the main idea.

Formula: Topic + what the author says about it = Main idea.  e.g. sleep problems + can be avoided by following a few simple guidelines → “Sleep problems can be avoided if you follow a few simple guidelines.”

Finding the main idea of a passage (from the slides). Ask yourself:

  • What idea is common to most of the text?
  • What is the idea that relates the parts to the whole?
  • What opinion do all the parts support?

Paragraphs usually have 2 types of sentences: a topic sentence, which contains the main idea, and one or more detail sentences.

Rules for finding the topic sentence (from the slides)

  1. The topic sentence is usually first, but it could be in any position in the paragraph.
  2. A topic sentence is more general than the other sentences: it looks at the big picture and sometimes refers to more than one thing. Plurals and the words “many”, “numerous”, “several” often signal a topic sentence.
  3. Detail sentences are more specific: they talk about one small part or side of an idea. “For example”, “i.e.”, “that is”, “first”, “second”, “third” … “finally” often signal a detail.
  4. Most detail sentences support, give examples, prove, talk about or point toward the topic in some way.

Where can the topic sentence be?

Firstmost common
Middleafter an introduction (“Some people fall asleep easily… Fortunately…”)
Lastas a summary or conclusion (“As can be seen…”)
First and laststated, then repeated
Never a questionlook for the answer to the question
Nowherean implied main idea

Implied main idea (from the slides)

  • Some paragraphs have no topic sentence, but they still have a main idea.
  • The supporting details suggest the main idea. It is implied (unstated), so you figure it out yourself.

How to create it

  1. What is the topic? Who or what is the whole paragraph about? See what subject is constantly referred to.
  2. What is the author’s main point about the topic?

Your answer should be…

  • not too general, not too specific
  • broad enough to include all the supporting details
  • stated in a complete sentence

References, Substitution, and Ellipsis7 pts

General guide, not from class slides

Part I: explain what the words in bold type refer to (½ pt each, about 14 words).

The handouts you sent do not cover this part, so this is a general guide, not from class slides. It follows standard reading-course definitions (cohesion: reference, substitution, ellipsis).

The three tools writers use to avoid repetition

Reference (คำอ้างอิง)

A word that points to something already mentioned (or coming next).

Substitution (คำแทน)

A word that replaces a noun, verb or clause: one(s), do/does/did (so), so, not, the same.

Ellipsis (การละคำ)

Words are left out because the reader can recover them: “Pete will [bring more cash] too.”

TypeCommon wordsExample → what it refers to
Personal pronounshe, she, it, they, him, her, themThe rovers landed in 2003. They sent back data. → the rovers
Possessiveshis, her, its, theirThe company raised its prices. → the company’s
Demonstrativesthis, that, these, those, such, here, thereWater covers 70% of Earth. This supports life. → (the fact that) water covers 70% of Earth
Relative pronounswho, which, that, whose, where, whenthe algae which live inside them → the algae
Comparative / orderthe former, the latter, the first, the last, another, the other(s)Tea and coffee… the latter → coffee
Nominal substitutionone, ones, the samea black T-shirt and a white one → T-shirt
Verbal substitutiondo, does, did, do so, do itShe told him not to lend money, but he did so anyway. → lent money
Clausal substitutionso, not (after think, hope, believe, say, be afraid)Is it free? I think so. → (I think that) it is free
Ellipsis(something omitted, often after will, can, do, too, either)Some reefs recover; others do not. → others do not recover

Direction: most reference words point back (anaphoric). Some point forward (cataphoric): “Her advice is this: start early.” Here this = start early.

Method: 4 steps for every bold word

  1. Identify the typePronoun, demonstrative, relative, substitute or a gap (ellipsis)?
  2. Look back firstUsually the same or the previous sentence. For a colon or “the following”, look forward.
  3. Match grammarSingular or plural, person or thing, noun or whole idea?
  4. Substitute and rereadPut your answer in place of the bold word. Does the sentence still make sense?
Exam strategy (7 pts, ½ each): write the full noun phrase (“the tiny algae”, not “algae” if the text is specific; “Nok and her brother Pete”, not “Nok”). For this/that/it that point to an idea, paraphrase the whole idea. For substitution and ellipsis, write the missing words (“do so” = lend her some money). Never leave a blank: ½ point each adds up quickly.
Traps: the nearest noun is not always the answer (“The manager thanked the workers because they had finished early” → the workers); its (possessive) ≠ it’s (it is); that may be a relative pronoun (points to a noun) or a conjunction (points to nothing).

The way a writer arranges ideas: Listing, Sequence, Comparison/Contrast, Cause/Effect.
Patterns
Listing
A generalization plus a list of details or examples. Key words: many, several, a number of, a variety of, a few, kinds of.
Patterns
Sequence
Events in time order or steps in a process. Key words: began, account, story, process, history, sequence.
Patterns
Series of events
Sequence type: things that happened one after another (dates, then, later).

Patterns

Shows how two things are similar and/or different (may include both, or only similarities).

Shows only the differences between two things. Signals: however, but, on the other hand, although, while, in contrast, than, conversely, yet, unlike.

One event or action causes another. Key words: causes, leads to, results in, is due to, is the result of, brings about…

Last-minute cheat sheet

One page for the night before.

Exam map (35 pts)

  • Skim & Scan 6: 6 headings + 6 chart blanks (½ each)
  • Paragraphs 7: MC topic/main idea (1 each) + write topic, MI, pattern (3)
  • Reference 7: about 14 bold words (½ each)
  • Comprehension 15: about 40% of your time

Skim vs scan

  • Skim = gist: first and last sentences → headings
  • Scan = one fact: predict its form (year, number, name) → chart
  • Headings: not too narrow (one detail), not too broad (whole article). 2 extras.
  • Chart: short answers, copy the text's words.

Topic · main idea · details

  • Topic = phrase (who/what)
  • Main idea = topic + author's point, a complete sentence
  • Topic sentence is general: plurals, many, numerous, several
  • Details are specific: for example, i.e., that is, first, finally

Where is the topic sentence?

  • First (most common) · middle (after an intro) · last (summary: “As can be seen”) · first + last
  • Never a question: find its answer
  • None = implied: topic? + author's point? → one complete sentence covering all details

Patterns + key words

  1. Listing: many, several, a number of, a variety of, a few, kinds of | for example, another, also, besides, in addition, finally
  2. Sequence (events / process): began, story, history, process | then, next, after that, in 1965, later, finally
  3. Comparison/Contrast: both, similar, differ, in common | similarly, like / however, while, on the other hand, in contrast, unlike
  4. Cause/Effect: causes, leads to, results in, is due to, is the result of, because of, as a result

Order matters → Sequence. Order doesn't matter → Listing. Contrast = differences only.

Reference toolkit

  • Pronouns, possessives, this/that/these/those/such, who/which/that
  • Substitution: one(s), do (so), so, not
  • Ellipsis: write the missing words
  • The former = 1st, the latter = 2nd
  • Method: type → look back → match number → substitute and reread
  • Write the full noun phrase or the whole idea

Comprehension question types

  • Main idea / title · detail · NOT mentioned
  • Vocabulary in context · reference
  • Inference (supported, not stated) · purpose / tone
  • Pattern · true / false / not given

Final tips

  • Read the questions before the passage
  • Prove each answer with a line in the text
  • Avoid extreme options (all, always, never)
  • Don't leave blanks: ½ points add up
  • Save 5 minutes to check headings are used once
